Common questions

Why do you double the cable length?

Current has to flow out to the load and back to the battery. Voltage drops along both the positive and the negative (or chassis return). A 5 m run to a fridge is really 10 m of cable. If you use the chassis as the return, make sure the earth strap is just as thick and the connections are clean and bare metal.

What voltage drop is acceptable?

The usual guide is 3 % (about 0.36 V on 12 V) for anything critical: lights, electronics, fridges, inverters. Up to about 10 % is tolerated for non-critical loads such as a winch motor for short bursts. For charging cables, keep it low (1–3 %) so the battery actually gets the voltage it needs.

Does the fuse go at the battery or at the load?

At the battery (the power source), within about 300 mm of the positive terminal. The fuse protects the cable that runs from it, so a fuse at the far end leaves the whole cable unprotected if it shorts to the bodywork.

Can I use a bigger fuse to stop it blowing?

No. A fuse that keeps blowing is telling you the load is bigger than the circuit. Never fit a fuse rated above what the cable can carry; fix the cause or fit thicker cable.

Is cheap “CCA” cable OK?

It works, but it has around 60 % more resistance than copper for the same size and carries less current, and it corrodes badly at crimps. Select CCA in the calculators to see the difference. For battery, inverter and charging cables, pure copper is worth the money.

What voltage should I design for?

For voltage drop on running circuits, 12 V is the safe assumption; with the engine running it is 13.8–14.4 V. For inverters, design for the low-battery current (around 10.5 V on a 12 V system), since that is when the inverter draws the most.

Series or parallel solar panels?

Series adds voltage, parallel adds current. Series keeps the current low, so the roof cable can be thinner and shading or long runs hurt less in voltage terms, but you must stay under the regulator's maximum PV voltage on a cold morning (panel voltage rises as it gets colder). Parallel is the right choice with a low-voltage PWM regulator or when partial shade is common.

Why isn't my caravan battery charging from the tow vehicle?
  • Voltage drop in a long, thin tow-plug cable (check it with the voltage drop calculator).
  • A smart alternator that only charges at ~12.5–13 V, so a basic relay or isolator won't fill the battery. Fit a DC-DC charger.
  • The vehicle's charge feed is on an ignition-switched circuit or a fuse that is too small.
  • A poor earth/return connection: the return path matters as much as the positive.
Do I need a DC-DC charger or can I wire the battery in directly?

On older vehicles with a fixed 14 V alternator, a direct fused feed through an isolator or relay can work, but the long cable still drops voltage. A DC-DC charger fixes both the smart alternator and long-cable problems, and gives your lithium or AGM battery the correct charge profile. It is the recommended method for caravans and campers today.

What about 24 V trucks?

Everything here works for 24 V: set the system voltage to 24. The same power needs half the current, so cables and fuses can be much smaller. A 24 V system is far kinder to voltage drop.

Safety: these tools give estimates to help you plan. A 12 V battery can deliver hundreds of amps into a short circuit, enough to melt cable and start a fire. Always fuse every positive feed close to the battery, use properly crimped lugs, and have large installs checked by a qualified auto-electrician. Check local regulations and the manufacturer's specifications for your equipment.
